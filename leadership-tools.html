Situational leadership: choose your style

Match your leadership style to a team member's competence and commitment on a specific task.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Pick one person and one task (not the person in general).
  2. 2Rate their skill on that task and their commitment (confidence and motivation).
  3. 3Read the recommended style and the first actions.
  4. 4Re-rate after a few weeks and move along the curve.

Use it when: Delegating, onboarding someone, or when a capable person suddenly under-performs.

Worked example

A new process engineer who is keen but has never run a trial.

Show the input values used
  • Skill on this task: Low: new to this task
  • Commitment (confidence and motivation): High: keen and confident
Use a Directing style (S1). High direction, low support. Tell, show, set clear steps and check often.
Recommended style
Directing
Direction
High
Support
Low
  • Define the task, standard and deadline in writing.
  • Demonstrate it once, then watch them do it.
  • Give feedback within a day.

How to read the result

  • The style belongs to the task, not the person: the same person may need Directing on one task and Delegating on another.

Formulas

  • Skill low + commitment high → Directing
  • Skill low/some → Coaching
  • Skill moderate/high + variable commitment → Supporting
  • Skill high → Delegating

Watch out for

  • Over-directing capable people lowers motivation.
  • Delegating to someone who is not ready sets them up to fail.

Source and basis

  • Drive: Principles of Management (MGTS ZC211) leadership session and #13 Leadership deck (Sem I–II); situational leadership as taught in those notes.
  • This is a simplified selector; discuss the style with the person rather than applying it silently.

Team capacity and workload

Compare the hours your team has with the hours your work requires, per person and overall.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the period length and each person's working hours.
  2. 2Enter the share of time lost to meetings and admin and the hours assigned.
  3. 3Read utilisation per person.
  4. 4Rebalance anyone above 90% or below 60%.

Use it when: Assigning work, planning a quarter, or deciding whether to hire.

Worked example

A five-person team over a 4-week period.

Show the input values used
  • Weeks in period: 4.00

  • Meetings, admin and breaks: 25.00 %
  • People: Asha / 0 / 112; Ben / 8 / 120; Chen / 0 / 90; Dev / 40 / 70; Eli / 0 / 128

How to read the result

  • Above 100% means someone is overcommitted on paper. Above 90% leaves no buffer.
  • Check the team total, but also each person: an average hides overload.

Formulas

  • Capacity = (weeks × hours/week − leave) × (1 − overhead%)
  • Utilisation = assigned / capacity

Watch out for

  • Do not plan at 100%: queues grow quickly as utilisation approaches 100%.
  • Skills matter. Spare hours of the wrong skill are not spare.

Source and basis

  • Standard capacity-planning practice, as in B.Tech ET ZC416 Production Planning and Control (capacity and utilisation).

Value of reusing knowledge (templates, playbooks, lessons)

Estimate the hours and money saved when a team reuses a proven template or playbook instead of starting from scratch.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter how often the task happens and the hours it takes from scratch.
  2. 2Enter the time saved with the template and how often it is actually used.
  3. 3Enter the one-time effort to build and maintain it.
  4. 4Read yearly savings and payback.

Use it when: Justifying time to document processes, create standard work or build a team library.

Worked example

A customer trial report done 40 times a year, 12 hours from scratch, 5 hours with a template.

Show the input values used
  • Times the task happens per year: 40.00
  • Hours from scratch: 12.00 h
  • Hours with the template: 5.00 h
  • Share of cases that really use it: 70.00 %
  • Cost per hour: 35.00 $
  • One-time build effort: 30.00 h
  • Yearly maintenance: 8.00 h
  • Quality benefit: fewer reworks: 20.00 h/yr
Saves about 208 hours a year (≈ $7,280). Payback in 1.7 months.

  • The biggest lever is the adoption share. A template nobody uses saves nothing.

How to read the result

  • Sensitivity: change the adoption share first. If the case only works at 100%, make adoption part of the plan.

Formulas

  • Saving = n × adoption × (hours scratch − hours template) + quality hours
  • Net = saving − maintenance
  • Payback = build / net × 12

Watch out for

  • Be honest about the time saved: measure it on two real cases.
  • Templates need an owner or they go stale.

  • Drive: your Knowledge Management and Management Information Systems course material (SECI model and knowledge reuse).

Change readiness (Kotter steps)

Score how ready your team is for a change across the eight Kotter steps and see which gaps to close first.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Think of one specific change.
  2. 2Score each of the eight steps from 1 (not in place) to 5 (strong).
  3. 3Read the overall score and the weakest steps.
  4. 4Work on the lowest scores in order; early steps matter most.

Use it when: Before launching a process change, a new tool or a restructuring.

Worked example

Introducing a new tooling standard across three plants.

Show the input values used
  • Score each step (1 to 5): 1 Create urgency / 4; 2 Build a guiding coalition / 3; 3 Form a strategic vision / 3; 4 Enlist a volunteer army / 2; 5 Remove barriers / 2; 6 Create short-term wins / 3; 7 Sustain acceleration / 2; 8 Anchor in culture / 1
Not ready: average 2.5. Starting now risks failure.
Readiness score
50 / 100
Weakest steps
8 Anchor in culture; 4 Enlist a volunteer army; 5 Remove barriers
  • Kotter's steps are sequential: weak urgency and coalition (steps 1–2) undermine everything after them.

How to read the result

  • Fix the earliest low score first, not simply the lowest.

Formulas

  • Readiness = average score × 20

Watch out for

  • Scores are opinions. Ask two or three colleagues to score independently and compare.

Source and basis

  • Drive: your Managing Change / Organisational Behaviour material; Kotter's eight steps, as also documented in the Core and Transformation frameworks pages of this site.

KPI scorecard with traffic lights

Turn a list of KPIs with targets and actuals into a one-page scorecard with weighted overall performance.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1List each KPI with target, actual and weight.
  2. 2Say whether higher or lower is better.
  3. 3Read the status per KPI and the weighted score.
  4. 4Focus on the red items.

Use it when: Weekly or monthly reviews for a team, plant or project.

Worked example

Five operational KPIs for a machining cell.

  • Each KPI is capped at 120% in the weighted score so one great result cannot hide a miss.

How to read the result

  • Look at the reds first, then the weights: a red KPI with a high weight deserves the next meeting.

Formulas

  • Higher is better: performance = actual / target
  • Lower is better: performance = target / actual
  • Weighted score = Σ min(performance, 120%) × weight

Watch out for

  • Too many KPIs hide the important ones. Aim for five to eight.
  • Agree definitions before the first review, or you will debate data instead of actions.

Source and basis

  • Balanced-scorecard practice as in your Principles of Management and Operations Management courses.

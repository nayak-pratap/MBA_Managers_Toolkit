Story planner (StoryBrand seven parts)

Build a clear message in seven parts, with your audience as the hero and you as the guide, and get a one-line statement you can say out loud.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Decide who the message is for. They are the hero of the story, not you.
  2. 2Fill in the seven parts: the character and what they want, their problem, you as guide, the plan, the call to action, what failure looks like, and what success looks like.
  3. 3Read the score. Anything marked missing is a gap the listener will feel.
  4. 4Copy the one-line statement and test it on someone who does not know your work.

Use it when: Writing the "about" section of a profile or website, opening a proposal, or answering "what do you do?".

Worked example

An engineering leader explaining a knowledge-management offer to manufacturing leaders. The percentages are the kind of result you would quote from your own work; replace them with yours.

Show the input values used

  • …and what they want: want proven machining solutions in front of customers faster
  • 2 · Their external problem: their engineering knowledge is scattered, so teams solve the same problem twice
  • …the internal problem (how it makes them feel): Frustrated that hard-won experience keeps walking out of the door
  • …the philosophical problem (why it is wrong): Knowledge a company has already paid for should not have to be paid for twice
  • 3 · You as guide: empathy: I have led a solutions engineering team through exactly this problem
  • …and authority: Built an AI-assisted knowledge system that cut search time by about 90% and revision hours by about 60%
  • 4 · The plan (the steps to work with you): Map where knowledge lives and where time is lost; Build one searchable, structured home for it; Add an AI copilot and reuse measures so it keeps improving
  • …the plan in one sentence: I help them put that knowledge in one searchable, AI-assisted system

Your one-line statement

Manufacturing and engineering leaders want proven machining solutions in front of customers faster, but their engineering knowledge is scattered, so teams solve the same problem twice. I help them put that knowledge in one searchable, AI-assisted system, so engineers find proven answers in minutes.

Built from the character, the problem, the plan and the success, the four components the book uses for a one-liner.

How to read the result

  • The score counts parts, not quality. A part can be "in place" and still be vague, so read each line as your audience would.
  • The one-line statement should make a stranger say "tell me more". If they ask "what does that mean?", make the problem more concrete.
  • Use the direct call to action as your main button or closing line, and the transitional one beside it.

Formulas

  • Completeness = number of the seven parts that are filled in
  • Part 2 needs the external and the internal problem; part 3 needs empathy and authority; part 4 needs 3 to 6 steps
  • One-line statement = character + want, "but" problem. Plan, "so" success.

Watch out for

  • Making yourself the hero. In this framework the customer or reader is the hero and you are the guide.
  • Selling only the external problem. The book argues people act on the internal one.
  • Several calls to action of equal weight. Choose one direct action.

Source and basis

  • Drive: 12_Communication_Storytelling_and_Career › Building a StoryBrand (Donald Miller, 2017). Read for this tool: the seven-part framework and its seven principles, the process-plan guidance (three to six steps), the two kinds of call to action, and the four-component one-liner.
  • Example values are illustrative.

Sticky idea checker (six principles)

Score a message against the six principles from Made to Stick and see which one to fix first.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Write the message exactly as you would say it.
  2. 2Score each principle 0, 1 or 2 using the question under it. Be strict.
  3. 3Read the total and the weakest principles.
  4. 4Rewrite the message using the suggested fix, then score it again.

Use it when: Before a presentation headline, a change announcement, a proposal summary or any message people must remember and repeat.

Worked example

A one-line message for a knowledge-search project, scored honestly: clear and concrete, but with no story in it yet.

  • Simple: 2 · Strong
  • Unexpected: 1 · Partly
  • Concrete: 2 · Strong
  • Credible: 1 · Partly
  • Emotional: 1 · Partly
  • Story: 0 · Missing

What to fix, weakest first

PrincipleScoreHow to fix it
Story0 / 2Add a two-sentence story: someone had a problem, did this, and here is what happened.

Emotional1 / 2Tell it through one person, and say what the listener gains.

How to read the result

  • A message does not need all six to work, and the book says so. It does need "Simple", and usually two or three of the others.
  • A low "Emotional" or "Story" score is normal for technical messages and is usually the cheapest to fix: add one person and what happened to them.
  • Ask a colleague to score the same message without seeing your scores. The difference shows what you take for granted because you know the subject too well.

Formulas

  • Stickiness = sum of the six scores (each 0 to 2), maximum 12
  • Verdict: 10 or more is sticky, 7 to 9 partly, below 7 unlikely (these bands are a working rule for this tool, not from the book)

Watch out for

  • Scoring your own message generously. You already know what you mean; the listener does not.
  • Treating "Simple" as "short". A short slogan that is not the core idea scores 0.
  • Adding statistics to raise "Credible". One concrete detail usually does more.

Source and basis

  • Drive: 12_Communication_Storytelling_and_Career › Made to Stick (Chip Heath and Dan Heath, 2007). Read for this tool: the introduction (six principles and the checklist scorecard) and the "Simple" chapter. Drive returned only the first part of this file, so the fix suggestions for the other five principles follow the book's introduction summaries.
  • The 0 to 2 scale and the verdict bands are this tool's own.

Pitch builder (four phases, 20 minutes)

Plan a short pitch in four timed phases and build the "big idea" statement, following the structure in Pitch Anything.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the time you have. The book plans for about 20 minutes.
  2. 2Fill in your track record, the three "why now" forces and the six slots of the idea statement.
  3. 3Add your key advantage and what you are offering.
  4. 4Read the time plan and the idea statement, then rehearse against the clock.

Use it when: Pitching a project for funding, proposing a new initiative to leadership, or presenting yourself for a role.

Worked example

An internal pitch for an AI knowledge copilot for a solutions engineering team. Replace every line with your own.

Show the input values used

  • Why now: economic force: Customers expect quotations in days, and engineering hours are the main cost of each proposal.
  • Why now: social force: Experienced engineers are retiring or moving, and new engineers expect to search, not to ask.

Your "why now" opening

Three things have changed. First, customers expect quotations in days, and engineering hours are the main cost of each proposal. Second, experienced engineers are retiring or moving, and new engineers expect to search, not to ask. Third, AI copilots can now search our own documents securely inside the tools we already use.

Your idea statement

For solutions engineers who are dissatisfied with searching old projects by memory and email, my idea is an AI knowledge copilot that provides a proven starting solution for any new enquiry in minutes. Unlike shared folders and personal notes, it answers in plain language, links to the source project and learns from every closed job.

The six-slot pattern from the book: for, dissatisfied with, category, provides, unlike, key features.

How to read the result

  • The time plan scales the book's 5 / 10 / 2 / 3 minute split to the time you have.
  • Say the "why now" opening before the idea statement. It shows the idea is moving with the market and is not an old plan.
  • Phase 4 is where the decision is made. Do not spend it repeating phase 2.

Formulas

  • Phase time = time available × (5, 10, 2, 3) ÷ 20
  • Idea statement = For [users] who are dissatisfied with [current way], my idea is a [category] that provides [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], it [key features].

Watch out for

  • Explaining every detail in phase 2. The book's point is control of attention and time, not completeness.
  • The book is written for deal-making with investors and its tone is combative. For internal or interview pitches use the structure and timing, and leave the status games out.
  • Skipping the offer. People cannot say yes to something that was never stated.

Presentation and data-story planner

Turn a before-and-after number into a headline people remember, then check the presentation against the course framework: hook, purpose, two to four points, summary and call to action.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the measure, its value before and after, and how often it happens.
  2. 2Read the conversions: percentage, "times faster", and hours or days per year. Choose the one your audience will feel.
  3. 3Fill in the audience, purpose, main points and the action you want.
  4. 4Check the framework list, then copy the outline.

Use it when: Preparing a results presentation, a project review, a business case summary or an exam presentation answer.

Headline options

Time to find a proven past solution fell 90%: from 30 to 3 min. Time to find a proven past solution: 10 times lower than before. That is about 108 working days a year given back to a team of 5.

Outline

TITLE: Three minutes, not thirty: what the knowledge system changed AUDIENCE: Engineering leadership; they know the project, not the results PURPOSE: to persuade HOOK: That is about 108 working days a year given back to a team of 5. POINT 1: Engineers now start from a proven solution Evidence: Search time 30 → 3 minutes POINT 2: Fewer revisions reach the customer Evidence: Revision hours down about 60% POINT 3: The knowledge stays when people move Evidence: 1,000+ solutions captured and reused SUMMARY: Time to find a proven past solution fell 90%: from 30 to 3 min. CALL TO ACTION: Approve the rollout to the Europe and US teams this quarter

  • Time guide for 10 minutes: about 1.5 min introduction, 7.0 min body (2.3 min per point), 1.5 min summary and call to action. The 15 / 70 / 15 split is a common rule of thumb, not part of the course framework.
  • Slides: the course guideline is the 6 × 6 rule, no more than six words per line and six lines per slide.

How to read the result

  • Pick one form of the number. A percentage suits a technical audience; "working days a year" suits people who manage budgets and people.
  • Put the headline number in the hook and repeat it in the summary. Everything between should explain why it is true.
  • If a point has no evidence, either find some or cut the point.

Formulas

  • Change % = (after − before) ÷ before × 100
  • Times lower = before ÷ after; times higher = after ÷ before
  • Per week = |after − before| × times per person per week × people
  • Per year = per week × working weeks; working days = hours ÷ 8

Watch out for

  • Multiplying a small saving by a large frequency you have not measured. Measure or sample the frequency, and say it is an estimate.
  • Time "saved" is not money saved unless the time is used for something else. Say what the time is now used for.
  • More than four main points. The course framework asks for two to four.

Negotiation prep sheet (one sheet and offer ladder)

Prepare a negotiation on one sheet and calculate a four-step offer ladder, following Never Split the Difference.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Choose whether you are buying or selling, and enter your target price.
  2. 2Write a two-sentence summary of the facts, then three to five labels and three to five open "what" or "how" questions.
  3. 3List non-cash items the other side could give that are valuable to you.
  4. 4Read the offer ladder and the checklist. Take the sheet into the meeting.

Use it when: Before a supplier or contract negotiation, a salary or role discussion, or any conversation where price or terms will be bargained.

Worked example

Buying a three-year software and training contract. The supplier quoted 1,650,000; the buyer's target is 1,200,000. Figures are illustrative.

Show the input values used
  • Your role: I am buying (I want a lower price)
  • Your target price (your goal): 1,200,000
  • Their opening price (0 if unknown): 1,650,000
  • Summary of the known facts (a couple of sentences): We have used their software for three years and renewal is due in six weeks. Their quote is up 22% and adds modules we did not ask for.
  • Labels: name what they may feel or accuse you of (three to five): It seems like you feel we only care about price and not about your support.; It sounds like the new modules matter a lot to your targets this year.; It looks like you think we will renew whatever the price is.
  • Calibrated questions (three to five): What matters most to you in this renewal?; How would I justify this increase to our finance team?; What would need to be true for a three-year price to work for you?
  • Non-cash items they could give: Two extra training days a year; A named support engineer; Price fixed for the full three years

  • Steps shrink on purpose (20, 10, then 5 points of target). Smaller and smaller moves signal that you are near your limit.

How to read the result

  • The ladder is a plan for your offers, not a script. Between each step use labels and questions to get the other side to move first.
  • The first offer will feel extreme. That is its purpose: it anchors the range. Deliver it calmly and with a reason.
  • A precise final figure reads as calculated. A round one reads as a guess that can be pushed.
  • Non-cash items often close the gap more cheaply than price.

Formulas

  • Buyer offers = target × 65%, 85%, 95%, 100%
  • Seller offers (adaptation) = target × 135%, 115%, 105%, 100%
  • Precise final figure = target adjusted by about 0.2% to a non-round number
  • Gap = |their opening − your target|

Watch out for

  • Using an extreme anchor in a long-term relationship without empathy. The book pairs the ladder with a lot of listening.
  • Setting the target at your bottom line. The book asks you to write down the best case.
  • Questions that start with "Why" tend to sound like accusations.
  • This is a preparation aid. Follow your company's purchasing rules and approval limits.

Source and basis

  • Drive: 12_Communication_Storytelling_and_Career › Negotiating › Never Split the Difference (Chris Voss with Tahl Raz). Read for this tool: the Ackerman bargaining steps, the appendix "Prepare a Negotiation One Sheet" (five sections), and the chapters on labels and calibrated questions.
  • The example labels and questions are written for this tool. Figures are illustrative.

Influence map (stakeholders without authority)

Map the people who can help or block a change, rank who to speak to first, and get an approach for each based on How to Win Friends and Influence People.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1List each person or role. Score their power over the outcome and their interest in it from 1 to 5.
  2. 2Choose where they stand today, from blocker to champion.
  3. 3Write what each one cares about, in their words.
  4. 4Read the priority order and the approach, and plan the first three conversations.

Use it when: Before proposing a change that needs support from people who do not report to you: a new tool, a process change, a cross-team project.

Worked example

Introducing an AI knowledge tool across engineering teams. Roles are generic; replace them with your own.

  • Priority is highest for powerful people who are against or undecided. Champions rank low because they need less of your time, not because they matter less.

How to read the result

  • Start at the top of the list. One conversation with a powerful sceptic is worth more than five with people who already agree.
  • The approach column gives the principle to lean on. The words must be yours and must be sincere; the book is clear that flattery fails.
  • Update the stance after each conversation and watch the power totals move.

Formulas

  • Priority = power × (3 − stance) + 0.5 × interest for people who are neutral or against (stance from −2 blocker to +2 champion)
  • Grid: power 4–5 and interest 4–5 = manage closely; high power only = keep satisfied; high interest only = keep informed; otherwise monitor
  • Power supporting / opposing / undecided = sum of power scores in each group

Watch out for

  • Scoring formal rank as power. Score power over this decision, which may sit with an expert or a gatekeeper.
  • Treating the map as a way to work around people. It is a way to understand what they need.
  • Keeping names and stances in a shared file. Treat a filled-in map as private.

Tolerance stack-up (worst case and RSS)

Add up the tolerances of parts in a chain to find the gap or length variation, with worst-case and statistical (RSS) results.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1List each part in the chain with its nominal size and ± tolerance.
  2. 2Mark each as + (adds to the gap) or − (subtracts).
  3. 3Read the nominal gap, worst-case range and RSS range.
  4. 4Compare with the minimum and maximum gap your design needs.

Use it when: You are checking whether parts will assemble, or deciding which tolerance to tighten.

Worked example

A shaft assembly: housing bore 50.00 ± 0.10 (+), spacer 12.00 ± 0.05 (−), bearing 20.00 ± 0.05 (−), shaft shoulder 15.00 ± 0.08 (−). The gap must stay between 2.0 and 3.5 mm.

  • Minimum gap needed: 2.00 mm
  • Maximum gap allowed: 3.50 mm
Every assembly fits, even at worst case: gap 2.720 to 3.280 mm.

  • RSS assumes the parts vary independently, are roughly centred and the process is capable. If you cannot show that, use worst case.

How to read the result

  • Worst case = every part at its limit in the unlucky direction. It is safe but expensive.
  • RSS accepts a small risk for looser tolerances. State the assumed capability when you use it.
  • Tighten the top row of the contribution table first: it is the cheapest improvement.

Formulas

  • Nominal = Σ (sign · nominal)
  • Worst case = nominal ± Σ tol
  • RSS = nominal ± √(Σ tol²)
  • Share of variation = tol² / Σ tol²

Watch out for

  • The direction (+ or −) is the most common error. Draw the loop first.
  • Do not use RSS on a stack with fewer than 3 parts or with non-centred processes.
  • This is a one-dimensional stack. Angles and geometric tolerances need more advanced methods.

Source and basis

  • Drive: Mechanical Tolerance Stackup and Analysis (Fischer); B.Tech ET ZC341 / ENGG ZC242 Engineering Metrology notes.

Shaft and hole fit (clearance or interference)

Find the minimum and maximum clearance or interference between a shaft and hole and classify the fit.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the nominal diameter.
  2. 2Enter the hole and shaft limits as deviations from nominal (upper and lower).
  3. 3Read max and min clearance.
  4. 4Check the fit type against what the application needs.

Use it when: You are choosing or verifying a fit on a bearing seat, pin, bush or press fit.

Worked example

A 25 mm hole with limits +0.021 / 0 and a shaft with limits −0.007 / −0.020 (a sliding fit).

Show the input values used
  • Nominal diameter: 25.00 mm
  • Hole upper deviation: 0.021 mm
  • Hole lower deviation: 0 mm
  • Shaft upper deviation: -0.007 mm
  • Shaft lower deviation: -0.020 mm
Clearance fit: from 0.007 mm (negative = interference) to 0.041 mm.

  • Clearance fit: parts always slide. Interference fit: parts are always tight (press or shrink). Transition: either can occur.

How to read the result

  • Positive minimum clearance means every assembly slides.
  • Negative maximum clearance means every assembly needs force or heat.

Formulas

  • Max clearance = hole max − shaft min
  • Min clearance = hole min − shaft max

Watch out for

  • Deviations are from nominal and carry a sign.
  • Thermal growth and surface finish can change a fit in service.

Source and basis

Reliability, MTBF, MTTR and availability

Compute failure rate, MTBF, availability and the probability a machine survives a mission time, for single items or series and parallel systems.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter operating hours, failures and total repair time to get MTBF, MTTR and availability.
  2. 2Enter a mission time to get reliability R(t).
  3. 3Add components in series or parallel to see system reliability.
  4. 4Read what the numbers mean for maintenance and spares.

Use it when: You need to quote uptime, size spares, or show why redundancy helps.

Worked example

A machine ran 4,000 h with 5 failures and 30 h total repair. Mission time 200 h. System: three units in series with R = 0.95, 0.98, 0.99.

Show the input values used
  • Operating hours: 4,000 h
  • Number of failures: 5.00
  • Total repair time: 30.00 h
  • Mission time: 200.0 h
  • System layout: Series (all must work)
  • Component reliabilities (0 to 1): Unit A / 0.95; Unit B / 0.98; Unit C / 0.99
MTBF
800 h

Availability

Failure rate λ
1.25 per 1,000 h
Reliability over 200 h
77.9 %

92.17 %
  • Series systems are weaker than their weakest link. Parallel systems are stronger than the best unit.
  • Assumes a constant failure rate (the flat part of the bathtub curve).

How to read the result

  • MTBF is not a guaranteed life. At MTBF only ~37% survive.
  • Availability improves by lengthening MTBF or shortening MTTR; repair speed is often the cheaper lever.

Formulas

  • MTBF = operating hours / failures
  • MTTR = repair hours / failures
  • A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
  • R(t) = e^(−t/MTBF)
  • Series: R = Π Rᵢ; Parallel: R = 1 − Π(1 − Rᵢ)

Watch out for

  • Count only failures in the same operating context and with a consistent definition of "failure".
  • Wear-out failures do not follow a constant rate.

Source and basis

  • B.Tech: ENGG ZC242 MTTF / MTBF / MTTR notes and ET ZC434 Quality, Reliability and Maintenance reliability lecture (Sem VII).

Process capability (Cp, Cpk, ppm)

Turn your measured mean, spread and specification limits into Cp, Cpk, expected defect rate and sigma level.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the lower and upper specification limits.
  2. 2Enter the process mean and standard deviation (or paste data summary).
  3. 3Read Cp, Cpk, ppm out of tolerance and sigma level.
  4. 4Use the shift target to see the benefit of centring.

Use it when: Before a launch, during a PPAP, or when a customer asks for Cpk.

Worked example

A bore specified 25.000 ± 0.050 mm with process mean 25.010 and standard deviation 0.012.

  • Upper spec limit: 25.05
  • Process mean: 25.01
  • Process standard deviation: 0.012

  • The ppm figure assumes a normal distribution and a stable process. Check a control chart first.

How to read the result

  • A large gap between Cp and Cpk means the process is off centre: re-centre before investing in variation reduction.
  • If Cp itself is low, you need to reduce variation, not just shift the mean.

Formulas

  • Cp = (USL − LSL) / 6σ
  • Cpk = min[(USL − μ)/3σ, (μ − LSL)/3σ]
  • ppm = [Φ((LSL−μ)/σ) + 1 − Φ((USL−μ)/σ)]·10⁶

Watch out for

  • Cpk is meaningless if the process is not in control.
  • Use short-term (within) sigma for Cpk and overall sigma for Ppk.
  • Needs at least 30 well-spaced measurements for a reliable estimate.

Sample size and confidence interval

How many parts must you measure to estimate a mean or a defect rate within a chosen margin, and what range does your sample support?

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Choose estimating a mean or a proportion.
  2. 2Enter the spread (or expected rate), the margin you can accept and the confidence level.
  3. 3Read the sample size.
  4. 4Or enter an existing sample to read its confidence interval.

Use it when: Planning an inspection, a trial run or a measurement study.

Worked example

Estimate the mean diameter within ±0.005 mm at 95% confidence when σ ≈ 0.012 mm.

Show the input values used
  • Estimate a: Mean (measured values)
  • Standard deviation σ: 0.012
  • Margin of error (±): 0.005
  • Confidence level: 95%
Sample size needed
23
  • Halving the margin of error needs four times the sample.
  • For a proportion use a conservative expected rate near 50% if unsure.

How to read the result

  • Round up. If measuring is costly, accept a wider margin first.

Formulas

  • Mean: n = (z·σ / E)²
  • Proportion: n = z²·p(1−p) / E²

Watch out for

  • A sample is only valid if it is random and representative.
  • Small expected defect rates need large samples to see anything.

Source and basis

  • B.Tech: ET ZC434 quality lectures (process capability, control) in your Sem VII folder; Drive Six Sigma material already used in the Black Belt pages.

Speeds, feeds and power

Turn a cutting speed into spindle rpm, feed rate, metal removal rate, power, torque and cycle time for turning, milling or drilling.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Pick the operation: turning, milling or drilling.
  2. 2Enter the cutting speed, diameter and feed from your tool catalogue or trial.
  3. 3Enter the specific cutting force kc for the work material and your machine power.
  4. 4Read rpm, feed rate, removal rate and the power check. Change one value at a time.

Use it when: You are setting up a job, checking a recommendation, or testing whether a machine has enough power for a heavier cut.

How to read the result

  • Spindle speed is what you program (or the rpm limit you must stay under).
  • Metal removal rate is the productivity number. Doubling feed or depth doubles it; speed also scales it directly.
  • Power check under 80% is comfortable. Over 100% means the machine will stall or slow down.

Formulas

  • n = 1000·Vc / (π·D) [rpm]
  • Turning: Vf = f·n; MRR = Vc·f·ap [cm³/min]
  • Milling: Vf = fz·z·n; MRR = ap·ae·Vf / 1000
  • Drilling: MRR = (π·D²/4)·Vf / 1000
  • Pc = kc·MRR(mm³/min) / 6×10⁷ [kW]; P_motor = Pc / η
  • Torque = 9549·Pc / n [N·m]

Watch out for

  • kc changes with chip thickness and tool geometry. The catalogue value is for a stated chip thickness, so treat power as an estimate (±20%).
  • Turning at the end of a bar: rpm should follow the current diameter, or use constant surface speed.
  • Milling with a narrow radial width needs a higher feed per tooth to keep the real chip thickness.

Source and basis

  • Drive: Metal Cutting (Trent, 1991 edition), chapters on terminology and forces in metal cutting; and Manufacturing Processes for Engineering Materials (Kalpakjian and Schmid).
  • B.Tech: ET ZC235 Manufacturing Processes and the machining books in your Drive library (Machining and CNC Technology, Fundamentals of Metal Machining and Machine Tools).
  • Drive: In Cut Optimization – Establishing Operating Conditions (cutting-tool training deck: operating conditions, Engineered Productivity, Tp and Tc formulas). Default values are illustrative.

Tool life (Taylor equation)

Predict how tool life changes with cutting speed, find the speed for a target life, or work out your own tool-life exponent from two tests.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Choose what you want to find.
  2. 2Enter your reference test: the speed you ran and the tool life you got (minutes to your wear limit).
  3. 3Enter the exponent n (about 0.15 for HSS, 0.2–0.4 for carbide, 0.5–0.7 for ceramic), or let the tool find it from two tests.
  4. 4Read the new life or speed, then use the "what if" mode to see why speed is the most expensive lever.

Use it when: A customer wants more productivity, a new grade is being compared, or you must explain why a small speed increase shortens tool life a lot.

Worked example

A carbide insert lasts 15 min at 250 m/min with n = 0.30. How long will it last at 300 m/min?

  • Rule of thumb for n = 0.30: every +10% in speed cuts life by about 27%.

How to read the result

  • A steep drop in the chart means speed is an expensive lever for that grade.
  • In "what if" mode, check the third result: if metal per edge goes down, you made the tool less efficient.
  • The exponent n is the key number. Higher n means life is less sensitive to speed.

Formulas

  • V·Tⁿ = C (Taylor)
  • T₂ = T₁·(V₁/V₂)^(1/n)
  • V = C / Tⁿ
  • n = ln(V₁/V₂) / ln(T₂/T₁)
  • Life change for a speed change: (1 + Δ)^(−1/n)

Watch out for

  • Tool life must be defined by the same wear limit in every test (for example 0.3 mm flank wear).
  • n and C are valid only for one work material, grade, geometry, feed and depth.
  • The rule "a speed change changes life by about twice as much" in the deck holds when n is near 0.5. For carbide at n = 0.3 the effect is about 3.3×.

Source and basis

  • Drive: In Cut Optimization – Establishing Operating Conditions (cutting-tool training deck: operating conditions, Engineered Productivity, Tp and Tc formulas).
  • Drive: M. C. Shaw, Tool Life (chapter in your cutting-tool library) and Metal Cutting (Trent).
  • Default n = 0.30 is a typical carbide value, illustrative.

Engineered Productivity (best tool-life window)

Find the tool life for maximum production and for minimum cost, and the cutting speeds that go with them, from your tool-change time, tool cost and machine burden rate.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter n, the tool-change time, the cost of one cutting edge and the machine burden rate.
  2. 2Enter one known point: a speed you ran and the tool life it gave, plus the cutting time and non-cutting time per part.
  3. 3Read the two tool lives. Your best speed lies between them. This is the Engineered Productivity (EP) range.
  4. 4Check the cost curve: it is flat near the minimum, so you can favour output or tool cost with little penalty.

Use it when: You want a defensible answer to "how fast should we run this tool?" instead of choosing a speed by habit.

Worked example

Carbide with n = 0.30, 5 min tool change, $2.00 per cutting edge, $60/hour burden rate, ran 15 min at 250 m/min.

How to read the result

  • Tp (maximum production) is the shortest sensible tool life. Faster than this, tool changes steal more time than the speed gains.
  • Tc (minimum cost) is longer because it also counts the cost of the edge.
  • A cheaper edge or a higher burden rate pushes the best speed up; a longer tool change pushes it down.

Formulas

  • Tp = (1/n − 1)·TCT
  • Tc = (1/n − 1)·(t/M + TCT) with t/M in minutes (edge cost ÷ burden rate per minute)
  • Cost per part = M·(tm + tnp) + (tm/T)·(M·TCT + t)
  • V = C / Tⁿ, C = V₁·T₁ⁿ

Watch out for

  • If your real tool change is 5 min but the tool is only checked at the end of a batch, count the true downtime.
  • Using long tool life because "inserts are expensive" often raises the total cost per part.
  • A very long tool life can also mean the speed is too low to cover the machine burden rate.

Source and basis

  • Drive: In Cut Optimization – Establishing Operating Conditions (cutting-tool training deck: operating conditions, Engineered Productivity, Tp and Tc formulas). The Tp and Tc formulas are taken from that deck. They reproduce its published chart (for example 11.67 min for a 5 min tool change at n = 0.30, and about 14.5 min at $0.40 edge cost with a $20/hour burden rate).

Cost per part: current tool vs proposed tool

Compare two tools or parameter sets on what really matters: total cost per part, parts per hour, and the tool life the new option must reach to still win.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the machine burden rate, non-cutting time and yearly volume.
  2. 2Fill in Option A (current) and Option B (proposed): cutting time per part, tool life, edge cost and tool-change time.
  3. 3Read the cost per part and the yearly saving.
  4. 4Look at the break-even tool life for B. If your trials show B lasts longer than that, B wins.

Use it when: A customer asks "is the faster or more expensive insert really cheaper?", or you need numbers for a trial report.

How to read the result

  • Always compare total cost per part, not tool price or tool life on their own.
  • The break-even tool life tells you how much the new option can disappoint in production and still pay off.
  • A higher tool cost per part is fine if the machine-time saving is larger.

Formulas

  • Edges per part = tm / T
  • Machine cost = (M/60)·(tm + tnp)
  • Tool-change cost = (M/60)·(tm/T)·TCT
  • Tool cost = (tm/T)·edge cost
  • Break-even life of B: T = tmB·(m·TCTB + tB) / (CostA − m·(tmB + tnp)), m = M/60

Watch out for

  • Use the same wear limit and the same part quality when you measure tool life for A and B.
  • Do not forget non-cutting time. If it is large, a faster cut saves less than it seems.
  • Tool life measured in a short trial can be optimistic. Use the break-even figure as your safety margin.

Source and basis

  • Drive: In Cut Optimization – Establishing Operating Conditions (cutting-tool training deck: operating conditions, Engineered Productivity, Tp and Tc formulas). The cost model (burden rate, tool cost, tool change time) follows the Tc logic in that deck.
  • B.Tech: ET ZC235 Manufacturing Processes (machining economics) and ET ZC416 Production Planning and Control (costing of production time).

Cutting forces and shear angle (orthogonal cutting)

From a measured chip thickness and the two cutting force components, get the shear angle, friction, shear stress and specific cutting energy.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter uncut chip thickness, measured chip thickness, width of cut and rake angle.
  2. 2Enter the cutting force Fc and thrust force Ft from a dynamometer or a test.
  3. 3Read the shear angle, friction coefficient and shear stress.
  4. 4Compare the shear angle with the Merchant prediction to judge how the cut is behaving.

Use it when: You want to understand a cut, compare two tool geometries, or explain why rake angle changes power and forces.

  • Merchant prediction for the shear angle with your friction angle: 33.7° (measured 31.2°). The two rarely match exactly; a large gap means the simple theory is off for this cut.
  • Higher rake angle → higher shear angle → lower force and power, but a weaker edge.

How to read the result

  • A higher shear angle means a thinner shear zone and lower force. Sharp, positive-rake tools raise it.
  • μ above about 0.8 points to heavy friction or built-up edge. Look at coating and lubricant.
  • Shear stress should be roughly the shear strength of the work material at cutting temperature. If it is far off, recheck forces and chip thickness.

Formulas

  • r = t₀ / tc; tan φ = r·cos α / (1 − r·sin α)
  • β = α + arctan(Ft / Fc); μ = tan β
  • Fs = Fc·cos φ − Ft·sin φ; As = t₀·w / sin φ; τ = Fs / As
  • γ = cot φ + tan(φ − α)
  • Merchant: φ = 45° + α/2 − β/2; u = Fc / (w·t₀)

Watch out for

  • Orthogonal theory assumes a straight cutting edge and no side flow. Real turning is only approximately orthogonal.
  • Chip thickness must be measured on a representative chip, not a broken piece.
  • The sign of the rake angle matters: negative rake lowers φ.

Source and basis

  • Drive: Metal Cutting (Trent, 1991 edition), chapters on terminology and forces in metal cutting; and Manufacturing Processes for Engineering Materials (Kalpakjian and Schmid). (force analysis and the shear-plane model).

  • Real surfaces are rougher than theory because of built-up edge, vibration and tool wear. Allow a margin and confirm with a surface tester.

How to read the result

  • Ra grows with the square of feed: halving the feed cuts Ra to a quarter.
  • Doubling the nose radius halves Ra, but a large radius can cause vibration on slender parts.
  • This is the finish from geometry only. It is a lower bound.

Formulas

  • Ra ≈ f² / (32·r) (f and r in mm; ×1000 for µm)
  • Rmax ≈ f² / (8·r)
  • f_max = √(32·r·Ra)

Watch out for

  • Valid for feed smaller than the nose radius and for a stable cut.
  • Ra of milled surfaces follows a different pattern (cutter marks and runout).

Source and basis

  • Drive: Metal Cutting (Trent, 1991 edition), chapters on terminology and forces in metal cutting; and Manufacturing Processes for Engineering Materials (Kalpakjian and Schmid).
  • B.Tech: ET ZC235 Manufacturing Processes and Engineering Measurements (surface texture and measurement).

Watch out for

  • The 0.7·UTS·t·L rule is for estimates. Check the work done on thick sheet and high-speed punching separately.
  • k depends on material and tooling. Use measured values for production.

Customer value case for a better solution

Turn a cycle-time, tool-cost and scrap improvement into yearly savings, payback and a multi-year net present value you can show a customer.

Open the interactive calculator → Opens with the worked example already filled in, so you can compare your numbers against it.

How to use it

  1. 1Enter the yearly volume, machine rate and the old versus new cycle time.
  2. 2Enter old and new tool cost per part and scrap rates.
  3. 3Set how much of the time saving the customer can really bank, and the one-time cost of the change.
  4. 4Read the savings, payback and the cash-flow chart. Share the table.

Use it when: You are preparing a recommendation, a trial report or a business case for a customer or for your own management.

  • Run it again with scrap and banked share lowered to see how fragile the result is.

Formulas

  • Time saving = (old − new cycle)/60 × rate × volume × banked share
  • Tool saving = (old − new tool cost per part) × volume
  • Scrap saving = Δscrap rate × volume × part cost
  • Payback = one-time cost ÷ yearly benefit × 12
  • NPV = −one-time + Σ benefit/(1 + r)^t

Watch out for

  • Do not use the full machine rate on hours that are not sold or avoided.
  • Include the extra tool or holder cost; leaving it out is the most common mistake in trial reports.
  • The defaults are synthetic and only show how the tool works.

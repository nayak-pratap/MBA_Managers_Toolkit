These frameworks are not part of the Manager's Toolkit assignment. I chose them because business transformation is my specialisation and the direction of my career, and they answer questions that the four assignment frameworks do not: where to compete, how to create value, how to build capability, how to bring people along, and how to govern AI.

Where I link a framework to my own work, that is my reading of my experience through the lens. Where I have not used a framework formally, I say how I would use it.

Where to Compete

Four strategy lenses for deciding which industries, activities and businesses deserve effort.

01Porter's Five Forces

Michael E. Porter · Harvard Business Review, 1979 · Competitive Strategy, 1980

The idea

Industry profitability is not luck. It is shaped by five forces: the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of suppliers, the bargaining power of buyers, the threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors. The stronger the forces, the harder it is for firms to earn good returns. Analysing them shows where the industry's profit is going and where a firm can position itself to defend it.

Example: where a few large customers can switch suppliers easily, buyer power squeezes margin, so differentiation or switching cost becomes the strategic priority.

Why it matters in transformation

Transformation decisions usually start inside the firm. Five Forces forces the outside view: will AI change who holds power? Cheaper tools lower entry barriers for new competitors, and customers who can do part of the work themselves become stronger buyers. A transformation leader uses it to test whether a change defends profit or only adds cost. Its limits are that it is a snapshot rather than a plan, and it assumes clear industry boundaries, which AI is blurring.

In my work

A global engineering-solutions centre serves customers across regions who could, in principle, source tooling designs elsewhere. Responsiveness and solution quality are what keep that buyer power in check. I would use the framework to test which AI-enabled capabilities strengthen that position and which only match what competitors can also buy.

02Value Chain

Michael E. Porter · Competitive Advantage, 1985

The idea

A firm is a collection of activities. Primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) create and deliver the product. Support activities (firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement) enable them. Advantage comes from performing activities more cheaply, or in a more valuable way, than rivals do. The margin is the difference between the value created and the cost of doing the activities.

Why it matters in transformation

The Value Chain gives AI and digital projects a place to land. Instead of asking where to use AI, a manager asks which activity is the real bottleneck for cost or value, and whether AI changes it. It also highlights linkages: a change in design can alter manufacturing cost, so improving one activity in isolation can mislead.

In my work

Engineering solution design sits upstream of manufacturing and close to the customer's problem, so the quality of knowledge available at that step shapes cost and turnaround downstream. One way to read my knowledge system is as an investment in that single activity. I would use the value chain to choose where the next investment should go.

03BCG Growth-Share Matrix

Bruce Henderson · Boston Consulting Group, 1970

The idea

Place each business unit or product on two axes: market growth rate and relative market share. Stars have high growth and high share and need investment. Cash cows have low growth and high share and fund the rest. Question marks have high growth and low share, so they need a decision to invest or exit. Dogs have low growth and low share and usually release resources. The aim is a balanced portfolio in which mature businesses fund promising ones.

Why it matters in transformation

Transformation always competes for limited money and attention. The matrix gives leaders a blunt, fast way to say which parts of the portfolio fund change and which deserve a bet, and it guards against pouring money into a low-growth business because it feels familiar. It reduces a business to two variables, assumes share drives profit and defines markets narrowly, so it is a starting conversation rather than a verdict.

In my work

A solutions-engineering team handles a wide mix of projects, about 1,300 across many end markets. Classifying those segments by growth and by our share of the work would show which to grow, which to run efficiently and which to stop chasing. I have not done this formally; it is how I would use the matrix.

04Blue Ocean Strategy

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne · Harvard Business Review, 2004 · Book, 2005

The idea

Red oceans are crowded markets where rivals compete on the same factors. Blue oceans are uncontested space, created by making competition irrelevant. The method is value innovation: pursue differentiation and low cost together by questioning the industry's assumptions. Two tools do the work. The strategy canvas plots how firms compete on key factors, and the Four Actions Framework asks what to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise and Create relative to industry norms.

Why it matters in transformation

Digital transformation is often used to do the same things faster, which only helps in a red ocean. Blue Ocean pushes leaders to ask which factors the industry takes for granted, and which AI could remove or raise. It is especially useful for turning an internal capability into something customers value in a new way. Its evidence rests on selected success stories, and new space is soon copied.

In my work

An engineering-solutions function normally competes on technical depth and turnaround. The four actions would ask which parts of the solution process could be eliminated or reduced by capturing expertise once, and what to create, such as self-service access to proven solutions. That is close to the thinking behind the knowledge system, now framed as strategy.

How to Create Value

Four lenses for turning capability into offerings, and offerings into adoption.

05Business Model Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur · Business Model Generation, 2010

The idea

A business model describes how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value. The canvas puts it on one page in nine blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. The right-hand side faces the market and the left-hand side is the infrastructure that delivers. A change in one block usually forces change in others.

Why it matters in transformation

The canvas lets a technical leader describe a capability in the language of the business: who is it for, what do they get, how do we earn or save money, and what does it cost. It is a quick test of whether a transformation idea is a business case or only a technology.

In my work

My profile is a bridge from technical innovation to market-centric leadership, and the canvas is the tool for that bridge. For the knowledge system, the customers are internal teams, the value is faster and more consistent solutions, and the key resources are the knowledge assets and the platform. I would extend the same exercise to describe its value to external customers.

06Design Thinking

Popularised by IDEO and the Stanford d.school · see Tim Brown, Harvard Business Review, 2008

The idea

A human-centred, iterative way of solving problems. The commonly taught stages are Empathize (understand the people involved), Define (state the problem from their point of view), Ideate (generate many options), Prototype (build cheap versions) and Test (learn from real use). Teams loop back as they learn instead of moving in a straight line.

Why it matters in transformation

Many AI projects fail because they solve a problem users never had. Design thinking brings users in early, makes experiments cheap and normalises being wrong in small ways. It suits ambiguous problems where DMAIC is a poor fit because there is no process to measure yet. It can feel soft to data-driven teams, and workshops without follow-through produce ideas rather than change.

In my work

The Copilot agent and knowledge system were first built for the technology function and then redesigned to serve sales, design and application engineering. A tool that worked for one group had to be re-understood from other groups' point of view. Empathize and Test are the stages I would make explicit next time.

07Three Horizons of Growth

Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley and David White · The Alchemy of Growth, 1999

The idea

Growth comes from managing three horizons at once. Horizon 1 extends and defends the core business, which delivers today's results. Horizon 2 builds emerging businesses that will become tomorrow's engines. Horizon 3 creates viable options for the future, which are small, uncertain and cheap. Each horizon needs different goals, people and measures, and the common mistake is to judge Horizon 3 ideas by Horizon 1 standards.

Why it matters in transformation

An AI roadmap is a portfolio problem. Using AI to improve current processes is Horizon 1, new services built on captured knowledge are Horizon 2, and exploratory pilots are Horizon 3. The framework lets leaders fund all three without starving the core or letting experiments drift.

In my work

My own work spans all three. Process automation and dashboards improved the current business (Horizon 1). The knowledge system and agent opened a new capability (in effect Horizon 2). Agentic AI is where I am exploring options (Horizon 3). Naming the horizons would help me explain how much attention each one deserves.

08Technology Adoption Lifecycle

Geoffrey Moore · Crossing the Chasm, 1991 · building on Everett Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations, 1962

The idea

Adopters of a new technology fall into five groups by when they adopt: innovators (about 2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). Moore's insight is that there is a chasm between early adopters, who buy a vision, and the early majority, who want proven solutions and references. Many innovations stall there. Crossing it means focusing on one narrow group and delivering a complete solution.

Why it matters in transformation

Every transformation has a rollout plan, and it fails if it treats everyone the same. The model tells you who to start with, why early enthusiasm does not predict broad uptake, and why the second wave needs evidence, support and peers who have already succeeded.

In my work

Rolling out the knowledge system and agent across functions meant moving from the technology team's early use to teams that needed proof it would save them time. The measured gains in solution turnaround and knowledge search time were the reference evidence that majority groups look for.

How to Build Capability

Three lenses on making the ability to change part of how the organisation works.

09Dynamic Capabilities

David Teece, Gary Pisano and Amy Shuen, 1997 · Teece, 2007

The idea

Resources and skills that give advantage today can become rigid tomorrow. Dynamic capabilities are an organisation's ability to integrate, build and reconfigure its competences as conditions change. Teece (2007) breaks them into three: sensing opportunities and threats, seizing them by mobilising resources, and transforming by renewing the organisation. They explain why some firms keep adapting while others with equal resources do not.

Why it matters in transformation

The framework treats transformation as a repeated capability rather than a project. One successful AI rollout matters less than whether the organisation can sense the next change, decide quickly and reconfigure around it. It is also the theory behind this site's thesis: making expertise scalable is a way of building the capacity to reconfigure.

In my work

Building a team and a knowledge system from the ground up is capability building. Sensing was noticing the gap in how knowledge moved, seizing was designing and funding the system, and transforming was extending it to other functions. I see my next role as building that cycle at the level of a whole business.

10SECI Model of Knowledge Creation

Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi · The Knowledge-Creating Company, 1995 · Nonaka, 1994

The idea

Organisational knowledge grows through conversion between tacit knowledge (know-how held in people's heads and hands) and explicit knowledge (written and shareable). Four modes form a spiral: Socialization (tacit to tacit, learning by working alongside), Externalization (tacit to explicit, putting know-how into words and models), Combination (explicit to explicit, joining sources into new systems) and Internalization (explicit to tacit, learning by doing from documented methods). Each turn of the spiral raises the knowledge of the whole organisation.

Why it matters in transformation

AI makes explicit knowledge cheap to search and recombine, which raises the value of the step before it: capturing tacit expertise. SECI tells a leader that a knowledge system is not just a repository. It needs all four modes, including mentoring and practice, or it becomes a library nobody uses.

In my work

Cutting-tool application knowledge sits mostly with experienced engineers. The knowledge system and the Copilot agent are Externalization and Combination at scale: expertise becomes captured assets, then an agent that recombines them. Skill matrices and mentoring cover Socialization and Internalization.

11McKinsey 7S

Robert Waterman, Tom Peters and Julien Phillips, 1980 · popularised in In Search of Excellence, 1982

The idea

An organisation works well only when seven elements fit together. Three are hard and easy to change on paper: Strategy, Structure and Systems. Four are soft and slower to change: Shared values, Style, Staff and Skills. Shared values sit at the centre because they link everything else. Misalignment between any two elements, such as a new strategy with an old structure, is a common reason change stalls.

Why it matters in transformation

Before changing anything, a leader can use 7S as a diagnostic: where are we aligned, and where will the change collide? It is especially helpful for AI adoption, because the technology touches systems and skills quickly while the blockers often sit in style and shared values.

In my work

A new knowledge system changes Systems and Skills quickly, but its use depends on Style (do leaders ask for answers from the system?) and Shared values (do experts see sharing as part of the job?). I would run a 7S check before scaling a tool, to see which elements need to move with it.

How to Bring People Along

Two lenses on the human side of change: the individual and the wider network.

12ADKAR

Jeff Hiatt, Prosci · ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and Our Community, 2006

The idea

Organisations change only when individuals do. ADKAR describes five outcomes each person needs, in order: Awareness of why the change is needed, Desire to support it, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to put it into practice, and Reinforcement to sustain it. Change stalls at the first missing element, so diagnosing which one is missing tells you what to do next.

Why it matters in transformation

Kotter works at the level of the organisation and ADKAR at the level of the person, so the two complement each other. ADKAR also helps with a team labelled "resistant": resistance is often a missing element, such as knowledge or ability, rather than unwillingness.

In my work

Adoption of a new tool shows where people are stuck. Someone who understands why but cannot yet use it needs training and time, not more messaging. I have used a skill matrix to find capability gaps, and ADKAR adds the awareness, desire and reinforcement side to that picture.

13Stakeholder Mapping

Aubrey Mendelow, 1981 (power-interest grid) · R. Edward Freeman, Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach, 1984

The idea

List everyone who is affected by the change or able to affect it, then place each on a grid of power (ability to influence) and interest (how much it matters to them). High power and high interest: manage closely. High power, lower interest: keep satisfied. Lower power, high interest: keep informed. Lower power, lower interest: monitor. People move between boxes as the work progresses, so the map needs revisiting.

Why it matters in transformation

Transformation work is often stalled by someone who was never asked, such as a function that owns the data or a leader whose budget is affected. A stakeholder map makes the human landscape visible early, and it shows who belongs in the guiding coalition and who needs reassurance.

In my work

A global engineering centre has stakeholders across regions: sales teams who use the solutions, design and application engineers who contribute, IT and security teams who govern platforms, and senior leaders who fund them. Mapping them before extending the agent beyond one function would clarify whom to involve first.

How to Govern

One lens on keeping AI trustworthy as it scales.

14NIST AI Risk Management Framework

National Institute of Standards and Technology (USA) · AI RMF 1.0, January 2023

The idea

A voluntary framework for managing the risks of AI systems and building trustworthiness. It is organised around four functions. Govern is the cross-cutting culture, policy and accountability. Map establishes the context and risks of a given system. Measure assesses and tracks those risks with suitable methods. Manage prioritises and acts on them. The framework describes trustworthy AI as valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable, privacy-enhanced and fair.

Why it matters in transformation

In the AI Wars case from Session 02, governance emerged as the biggest gap between importance and preparedness. A transformation leader who can talk about AI risk in a structured way is more credible with legal, risk and the board. The framework also gives a practical checklist for pilots before they scale.

In my work

An internal agent that draws on company knowledge raises questions of accuracy, confidentiality and accountability. Govern sets who owns it, Map identifies where wrong answers would matter, Measure checks answer quality, and Manage defines how issues are handled. I would write that risk plan before extending any agent to a new function.

No project needs all fourteen. The skill is to start from the question and choose the lens. Each group pairs naturally with one of the four assignment frameworks.
